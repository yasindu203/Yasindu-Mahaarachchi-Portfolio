Philosophy

On Thinking in Systems

July 2025

There is a particular kind of clarity that comes from seeing the world not as a collection of objects, but as a web of relationships and flows. Supply chains taught me this. Not the textbook version — not the flowcharts and the SAP screenshots — but the actual lived reality of why a container sits at a port for six days, or why a warehouse runs out of a component that was theoretically in stock.

The answer is almost never a single broken link. It is almost always a cascade: a decision made in isolation, a signal that arrived too late, a buffer that existed on paper and not in practice. Systems thinking is the discipline of tracing those cascades backward to their roots, and forward to their second- and third-order consequences.

What I find beautiful about this is how transferable it is. Once you can see feedback loops, you see them everywhere — in financial markets, in career trajectories, in personal habits. The tools of operations research and the instincts of a good strategist are not as far apart as their vocabularies suggest.

This is what I am building toward: the ability to stand at the intersection of those vocabularies and translate fluently between them.

Journeys

Management Level — Completed

2025

The Management Case Study examination is not a test of memory. It is a test of judgment under pressure — the ability to read a scenario, identify what is actually being asked beneath the surface, and construct an argument that is both analytically sound and practically useful.

I scored 111 marks on the Management Case Study. I am recording this here not as a boast, but as a reference point: a marker of where the CIMA journey stood at a particular moment.

The path to this result was not linear. The Management Level covers Performance, Risk, and Financial management — three disciplines that feel separate in the syllabus and deeply interconnected in the real case study rooms. What prepared me most was not grinding past papers, but learning to write under exam conditions as if advising a real board: with conviction, with structure, and with the intellectual honesty to acknowledge where the data is ambiguous.

The Strategic Level is next. SCS — Strategic Case Study. The stakes are higher, the scenarios are more open-ended, and the expectation is that the candidate has a view, not just an answer.

I am reading for it now.

Why Engineering?

2024

I chose Transport Management and Logistics Engineering at the University of Moratuwa because it sits at an unusual intersection — rigorous enough to satisfy the part of me that likes proof and derivation, applied enough to connect to the real world of moving goods, people, and information at scale.

Engineering teaches you that constraints are not obstacles to creativity — they are the conditions of creativity. A bridge must not fall. A schedule must hold. A system must degrade gracefully when one node fails. These are not limitations on thinking. They are what makes thinking matter.

The first year was about foundations: mathematics, mechanics, programming. The second year introduced the domain: transport networks, logistics systems, operations research. I found myself most alive in the quantitative methods units — not because they were easy, but because they were demanding in the right way. They required building a model, questioning its assumptions, and sitting with uncertainty in a disciplined way.

This journey is the record of what I am building, discovering, and occasionally breaking along the way.
